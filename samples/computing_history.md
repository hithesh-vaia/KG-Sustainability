# A Short History of Early Computing

Charles Babbage, an English mathematician and inventor, designed the Difference
Engine in the 1820s to automate the calculation of polynomial tables. He later
conceived the Analytical Engine, a mechanical general-purpose computer that
included an arithmetic logic unit, control flow through conditional branching and
loops, and memory. The Analytical Engine was never completed during Babbage's
lifetime, but its design anticipated features of modern computers.

Ada Lovelace, daughter of the poet Lord Byron, worked closely with Babbage. In
1843 she translated an article on the Analytical Engine by the Italian engineer
Luigi Menabrea and appended extensive notes of her own. Her Note G described an
algorithm for computing Bernoulli numbers with the Analytical Engine, and is
often cited as the first published computer program. Lovelace also speculated
that such a machine could manipulate symbols beyond numbers, including music.

Nearly a century later, Alan Turing, a British mathematician, formalized the
notion of computation with the Turing machine in his 1936 paper "On Computable
Numbers." During the Second World War, Turing worked at Bletchley Park, where he
designed the Bombe, an electromechanical device used to break the German Enigma
cipher. After the war he contributed to the design of stored-program computers
and proposed the Turing Test as a criterion for machine intelligence.

The first general-purpose electronic digital computer, ENIAC, was built at the
University of Pennsylvania by John Mauchly and J. Presper Eckert, and became
operational in 1945. John von Neumann described the stored-program architecture
in the "First Draft of a Report on the EDVAC," an organization still known today
as the von Neumann architecture. These machines connected the theoretical work of
Turing and the earlier mechanical vision of Babbage and Lovelace to the practical
electronic computers that followed.
